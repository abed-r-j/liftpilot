## 1. The business problem

A retailer sends a message to its customers. Afterwards many of them buy. The dashboard says
"the campaign worked". The question the dashboard does not answer is: how many of those
customers would have bought anyway?

In our data a real grocery chain sent a communication to customers chosen at random.

- Contacted customers who bought: **63.65%**.
- Held-out customers (no message) who bought: **60.33%**.
- Difference caused by the campaign: **+3.32 points** (95% interval +2.90 to +3.75).

So 60.33 / 63.65 = 94.8% of the purchases that followed a message would have happened without
it. About 95 of every 100 "campaign purchases" were not caused by the campaign. A dashboard, or
a platform's reported ROAS, counts all 100.

The message still costs money for every customer, and a discount is paid to every buyer,
including the ones who would have bought at full price. The business problem is therefore:
**spend only on the customers whose behaviour the message changes, and prove that the plan
earns money before spending.**

LiftPilot answers three questions for a marketing manager:

1. Who should we contact, and who should we leave alone?
2. What will that plan really earn, with an honest margin of error?
3. Is the plan fair, and what should the messages say?

A human then approves or rejects the plan. The system never sends anything by itself.

## 2. What a randomised experiment is and why it matters

A randomised experiment (also called an A/B test or a randomised controlled trial) splits
customers into two groups **by coin flip**:

- the **treated** group gets the message;
- the **control** group (also called "held out") gets nothing.

Because a coin decided, the two groups are alike in every other respect: same mix of loyal and
occasional customers, same season, same prices. The only systematic difference is the message.
So the difference in purchase rate between the groups is the effect of the message. This
difference is called the **average uplift** (or average treatment effect).

Without randomisation you cannot do this. If the marketing team chose who to contact, the
contacted customers would differ from the others before any message was sent (for example they
would be the best customers), and "contacted customers buy more" would prove nothing.

In the X5 data, 99,981 customers were treated and 100,058 were held out. The difference of 3.32
points has z = 15.3, which means it is about fifteen standard errors away from zero: the
p-value is far below 0.001. The effect is real. It is also small.

Randomisation gives us a second gift that the whole project relies on: because *every* kind of
customer appears in both groups, we can later ask "what would have happened if we had contacted
only customers of kind A?" and read the answer from the experiment (section 10).

## 3. The data

**X5 RetailHero** (main dataset). Published by X5 Retail Group, a Russian grocery retailer, for
a 2019 competition. Three files:

- purchases: 45.8 million receipt lines, the purchase history **before** the campaign;
- clients: card dates, age, gender;
- uplift_train: for 200,039 customers, whether they were contacted (random) and whether they
  bought afterwards.

Numbers to know: 400,162 customers have purchase history; 200,039 were in the experiment; the
other 200,123 were never tested and are the "next audience" the agent plans for. The history
window is 2018-11-21 to 2019-03-19. The second date is the reference date from which recency
is counted, which is the day after the last receipt in the file.

Four things the source does **not** tell us, which you must not claim:

- the channel. The source says "a communication" and speaks of sending "a text to the client".
  Do not say SMS.
- the currency. We presume Russian roubles (RUB) because the retailer is Russian.
- the purchase amount after the campaign. Only "bought or not" is recorded. Our money figures
  value a purchase at that customer's own average basket (mean 463 RUB).
- the length of the outcome window.

**Hillstrom** (external check). An e-mail test published by Kevin Hillstrom in 2008: 64,000
customers of an online retailer, randomly sent a men's e-mail, a women's e-mail or nothing, with
visits and real spend in USD over two weeks. We treat "any e-mail" as contacted.

Raw data are not in the repository. `liftpilot download` fetches them and checks their SHA-256
fingerprints. Details: [DATASHEET.md](DATASHEET.md).

## 4. RFM step by step

RFM is the classic way marketers describe a customer with three numbers:

- **Recency**: days since the last purchase (fewer is better);
- **Frequency**: number of purchases in the window;
- **Monetary**: total amount spent.

We compute them from the raw receipts. One trap: the receipt total is repeated on every line of
a receipt, so lines are first collapsed to one row per receipt, otherwise Monetary is inflated.
Recency is counted from the day after the last date in the data, not from today.

**Scores.** Each of the three numbers is turned into a score from 1 (worst) to 5 (best) by
quintile: sort all customers, cut into five equal groups.

Illustration with five customers (with five customers each quintile holds exactly one):

| Customer | Days since last purchase | Purchases | Spend | R | F | M |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 2 | 40 | 17,000 | 5 | 5 | 5 |
| B | 5 | 18 | 3,000 | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| C | 9 | 30 | 16,000 | 3 | 4 | 4 |
| D | 20 | 15 | 9,000 | 2 | 2 | 3 |
| E | 60 | 3 | 1,000 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

A bought most recently, so R = 5; E bought longest ago, so R = 1. The same logic gives F and M:
for example B has the third-highest number of purchases (F = 3) and the second-lowest spend
(M = 2).

**Segments.** The scores are combined into eight named segments. The code first averages F and
M into one number, FM = (F + M) / 2, then applies these rules in order:

| Rule (first match wins) | Segment | In words |
|---|---|---|
| R >= 4 and FM >= 4 | Champions | bought very recently, buy often, spend the most |
| R >= 3 and FM >= 4 | Loyal | regular and recent |
| R >= 4 and FM >= 2.5 | Potential loyalists | recent, average frequency |
| R >= 4 | New customers | recent, few purchases so far |
| R = 3 and FM >= 2 | Need attention | average on everything |
| R <= 2 and FM >= 4.5 | Cannot lose them | were among the best, now inactive |
| R <= 2 and FM >= 3 | At risk | used to buy often, not lately |
| otherwise | Hibernating | low on every score |

In the illustration: A is a Champion (R 5, FM 5). B has R 4 and FM (3 + 2) / 2 = 2.5, a
Potential loyalist. C has R 3 and FM 4, Loyal. D has R 2 and FM (2 + 3) / 2 = 2.5, which is
below the 3 needed for "At risk", so Hibernating. E (R 1, FM 1) is Hibernating.

There is also a weighted RFM score (recency 0.40, frequency 0.35, monetary 0.25). These weights
are a stated assumption. The score is used only for the baseline rule "contact the best RFM
customers" and for the RFM curve in the Qini figure; it does not decide who LiftPilot contacts.

**What RFM showed on the real data** (section 3 of RESULTS.md). Because the experiment was
random, we can measure the real uplift inside each segment:

| Segment | Buys without contact | Uplift (points) | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Champions | 88.1% | +1.2 | +0.7 to +1.8 |
| Loyal | 80.2% | +1.2 | -0.1 to +2.6 |
| Potential loyalists | 70.4% | +3.3 | +2.2 to +4.4 |
| New customers | 53.5% | +5.6 | +3.8 to +7.4 |
| Need attention | 59.5% | +3.5 | +2.1 to +4.9 |
| At risk | 56.3% | +3.2 | +1.9 to +4.6 |
| Cannot lose them | 65.8% | +1.9 | -0.5 to +4.4 |
| Hibernating | 34.3% | +4.6 | +3.9 to +5.4 |

The sentence to say: **"Champions buy 88% of the time with no message, and the message adds 1.2
points. Hibernating and New customers gain 4.6 and 5.6 points. Rewarding your best customers
wastes budget."** The same pattern holds score by score: customers with frequency score 5 buy
89% of the time without contact and gain +1.3; frequency score 1 buys 29% of the time and gains
+4.5. The more often a customer already buys, the less a message adds.

Two segments (Loyal, Cannot lose them) have intervals that include zero: for them we cannot say
the message had an effect at all.

## 5. Uplift and the four textbook customer types

**Uplift** for one customer is

```
uplift = P(buy if contacted) - P(buy if not contacted)
```

It is the change the message causes. It is not the chance of buying. A Champion has a very high
chance of buying and a very low uplift.

Uplift for a single person can never be observed: a customer is either contacted or not, never
both. That is why we need the experiment (to see both situations across similar customers) and
a model (to estimate uplift for customers we have not tested).

**The textbook picture** sorts customers into four types:

| | Buys if contacted | Does not buy if contacted |
|---|---|---|
| **Buys if not contacted** | Sure things: contact is wasted | Sleeping dogs: contact does harm |
| **Does not buy if not contacted** | Persuadables: the only ones worth contacting | Lost causes: contact is wasted |

These names are common practitioner vocabulary. We do not attribute them to a specific paper:
our literature check could not confirm where the four labels are first defined, and they are
not in the Radcliffe and Surry (2011) report that is often cited for them.

**What our data did and did not show.** Types cannot be observed for an individual, so the code
does not label people with them. It forms three **decision groups** at the default costs and
then checks their real uplift on test customers the model never saw:

| Decision group | Share | Buys without contact | Real uplift | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| contact | 70.7% | 52.6% | +3.5 | +2.4 to +4.7 |
| skip: gain below cost | 16.1% | 72.3% | +3.7 | +1.5 to +5.8 |
| skip: no expected effect | 13.2% | 85.9% | +1.7 | -0.1 to +3.5 |

What this shows:

- A "sure things" pattern exists: the group the model expects no effect from buys 85.9% of the
  time without a message, and the message adds little (+1.7, interval includes zero).
- **No sleeping dogs were found.** The group with a non-positive estimated effect had a real
  effect of +1.7 points, not a negative one. Never say the system found customers who are
  harmed by contact.
- The group skipped because "gain below cost" has a real uplift (+3.7) that is not lower than
  the contacted group's (+3.5); the intervals overlap. The model separates customers only
  weakly at cheap contact costs. This is consistent with the 2 RUB result, where LiftPilot does
  not beat contacting everyone.

## 6. The learners

All learners use the same 33 behaviour features (RFM, basket statistics, activity in the last
7 days, the last 30 days and the 30 days before that, loyalty points, and so on) and the same
model family, gradient-boosted
decision trees (LightGBM). Age and gender are never inputs. The learners differ in how they
turn "treated" and "control" data into an uplift estimate.

- **Response model (the baseline).** Trained only on contacted customers to predict "will
  buy". This is the conventional likely-buyer model. Intuition: it finds people who buy, not
  people who change. It is the thing uplift modeling is meant to beat.
- **S-learner** ("single"). One model that takes the features plus a flag "contacted yes/no".
  To get uplift, predict twice for the same customer, once with the flag on and once off, and
  subtract. Intuition: ask one expert the same question in two worlds.
- **T-learner** ("two"). Two models, one trained on contacted customers and one on held-out
  customers. Uplift is the difference of their predictions. Intuition: two experts, one per
  world. Our pipeline also keeps this learner's two predictions: P(buy if contacted) is the p1
  of the profit rule (section 9), and P(buy if not contacted) is used to size the hold-out and
  to explain a single customer.
- **X-learner.** Starts like the T-learner, then for each contacted customer computes "what
  they did minus what the control model says they would have done" (and the mirror image for
  held-out customers), and trains a second pair of models directly on those differences.
  Intuition: model the effect itself instead of subtracting two noisy predictions.
- **DR-learner** ("doubly robust"). Builds for each customer a corrected effect score that
  combines model predictions with the actual outcome, using predictions made by models that did
  not see that customer (cross-fitting), then trains a model on that score. Intuition: the
  correction term protects the estimate when the first-stage models are imperfect.
- **Class transformation.** Creates a new yes/no label that equals 1 for "contacted and bought"
  or "not contacted and did not buy", and trains one classifier on it. With a 50/50 experiment,
  uplift = 2 x P(label = 1) - 1. Intuition: a trick that turns uplift into an ordinary
  classification problem.
- **Ensemble.** The average of the five uplift learners after putting their scores on a common
  scale (each score minus its mean, divided by its spread, both measured on the training
  customers). Intuition: the learners disagree customer by customer, so averaging cancels part of
  the noise.

The **champion** is the uplift learner (or the ensemble) with the best Qini on the calibration
split, never on the test split. The response model cannot be chosen. On X5 the champion is the
S-learner.

## 7. Qini: how to judge an uplift ranking

We cannot check uplift customer by customer, so we judge a model by its **ranking**.

**The Qini curve.** Sort test customers from highest to lowest score. Walk down the list. After
the first k customers, compute the extra purchases among them:

```
Q(k) = purchases of treated in top k  -  purchases of control in top k x (treated count / control count)
```

Plot Q against the share of customers contacted. Three lines matter:

- the **random line**: a straight diagonal from zero to the total effect. A model that ranks at
  random follows it.
- the **model curve**: a good uplift model climbs faster than the diagonal at the start, because
  it puts the persuadable customers first.
- a curve **below** the diagonal means the ranking is worse than random: it puts the least
  persuadable customers first.

**The Qini coefficient** is the area between the model curve and the random line, divided by
the same area for a theoretical perfect ranking. Zero means random. Positive means better than
random. Our values look tiny (around 0.009) because the perfect ranking is unreachable when 60%
of customers buy anyway and the average effect is 3 points. What matters is the sign and whether
the 95% interval excludes zero.

**Uplift at top 10%** is easier to say aloud: take the 10% of customers the model ranks highest
and measure their real uplift in the experiment.

How to read `fig_qini.png`: it draws three rankings of the test customers against the dashed
random line. The likely-buyer model and the RFM score stay below the diagonal for most of the
range, so they put the wrong customers first. The champion uplift model climbs above the
diagonal from the start and stays above it for almost the whole range.

## 8. Calibration

A learner's score orders customers reasonably but its size cannot be trusted. The profit rule
needs sizes: "is this customer's uplift above 2.1 points or not?".

Calibration fixes this on a separate split of customers (the calibration split, 40,008
customers):

1. sort them by score and cut into 20 equal bins;
2. in each bin measure the real uplift (treated rate minus control rate);
3. fit an **isotonic regression** through those 20 points, giving more weight to the bins that
   are measured more precisely. "Isotonic" means the fitted line can never go down as the score
   goes up, so no customer overtakes another. Customers on a flat stretch of the line get the
   same calibrated uplift.

After this step a score is translated into "customers with this score had about x points of
real uplift". `fig_calibration.png` shows the measured points and the fitted line.

Why three splits? **Train** (120,023) fits the models. **Calibration** (40,008) picks the
champion and calibrates it. **Test** (40,008) is used once, to report results. If the same
customers were used to choose and to grade, the grade would be too good.

## 9. The profit rule

For customer i:

- V = expected basket (their own average basket, pulled towards the overall average when they
  have few purchases);
- m = gross margin (share of revenue kept after the cost of goods);
- c = cost of one message;
- d = discount as a share of the basket (0 for a plain reminder);
- p1 = probability of buying if contacted (from the T-learner);
- uplift = calibrated uplift of the champion.

**Expected incremental profit of contacting i:**

```
gain = m x V x uplift  -  d x V x p1  -  c
```

The first term is the extra margin the message creates. The second is the discount, paid to
everyone who buys after contact, including those who would have bought anyway. The third is the
message.

**Contact only if gain > 0**, which is the same as

```
uplift  >  break-even uplift  =  (c + d x V x p1) / (m x V)
```

Illustration. Basket 400 RUB, margin 25%, so one extra purchase is worth 100 RUB of margin.

- Message at 2 RUB, no discount: break-even uplift = 2 / 100 = 2 points. A customer with 4
  points of uplift gives 100 x 0.04 - 2 = +2 RUB. Contact.
- Message at 6 RUB: break-even = 6 points. The same customer gives 4 - 6 = -2 RUB. Do not
  contact.
- Message at 2 RUB with a 3% discount, for a customer who buys 60% of the time when contacted:
  expected discount = 0.03 x 400 x 0.6 = 7.2 RUB; break-even = (2 + 7.2) / 100 = 9.2 points.
  The customer with 4 points of uplift now gives 4 - 7.2 - 2 = -5.2 RUB. Do not contact.

The discount term is why "sure things" are expensive: their uplift is near zero but they still
collect the discount.

On the real data the mean break-even uplift is 2.13 points at 2 RUB, 6.39 points at 6 RUB and
9.78 points with the 3% discount. Compare with the average uplift of 3.32 points and you can
predict the results: at 2 RUB most customers clear the bar; at 6 RUB most do not.

Before the rule is applied, some customers are removed by rule: customers on the opt-out list,
customers under 18, and (by default) customers with no positive expected effect. If the manager
sets a budget, customers are taken in order of profit per unit of spend until the budget is
used. A campaign can also be restricted to named RFM segments, and to nothing else: an
unknown segment name, or a demographic one such as "women over 60", is refused with an error.
The margin, the costs and the discount are **scenario assumptions**, not facts about X5.

## 10. Replay: evaluating a plan without running it

The model says a plan *should* earn x. How do we know what it *would really* earn? We replay it
on the experiment.

Take the 40,008 test customers, which the model never saw and whose contact was decided by coin
flip. Apply the plan's rule to them. This gives a set A of customers the plan would contact.
Inside A, some customers happened to be contacted in the experiment and some happened to be
held out. Then:

```
real incremental profit of the plan = (number in A) x ( average profit of contacted in A
                                                        - average profit of held-out in A )
```

where a contacted customer's profit is (margin - discount) x their revenue - message cost, and a
held-out customer's profit is margin x their revenue. The result is scaled to the size of the
audience.

Why this is honest:

- it uses real behaviour, not model predictions;
- it uses customers that played no part in training or calibration;
- it works for *any* rule (contact everyone, best RFM customers, likely buyers, LiftPilot), so
  all rules are compared on the same footing;
- it needs no new campaign, so the evidence exists **before** money is spent.

Its limits: revenue on X5 is imputed (a purchase is valued at the customer's average basket),
and one experiment in one period cannot promise the same effect next time. The technical name
of the estimator is "self-normalised inverse-propensity" (Hajek); you can say "replay on the
held-out randomised customers".

## 11. Bootstrap intervals

Every estimate is computed on a sample of customers, so it has sampling noise. The bootstrap
measures that noise without formulas:

1. draw a new sample of the same size from the test customers, **with replacement** (some
   customers appear twice, some not at all), separately in the contacted and the held-out group;
2. recompute the number (profit, Qini);
3. repeat 300 times (200 for the cost frontier of section 6 of RESULTS.md);
4. the 95% interval runs from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of those results.

How to read an interval:

- "204,762 RUB (95% interval -206,450 to 598,342)" means the best estimate is positive but a
  loss cannot be ruled out. Say so.
- "P(profit > 0) = 84%" is the share of the resamples in which the profit was positive. It is
  a summary of sampling noise, not a promise about the next campaign.
- For a **difference** between two rules we use a *paired* bootstrap: both rules are evaluated
  on the same resampled customers, so the interval describes the difference itself. A
  difference is significant when its interval excludes zero.

## 13. The agent, tool by tool

**What it is.** A language model that runs on the laptop (Qwen3, 8 billion parameters, served
by Ollama, no cloud, 0 USD) and can call 16 tools. In the course taxonomy it is a
**utility-based agent**: its goal is a number, incremental profit ("where the next 100 goes"),
and its tools let it compute, test and act on that number.

**The loop.** Read the manager's request, decide which tool to call, read the result, repeat,
then write an answer. The model never does arithmetic. Every fact comes from a tool, and the
tools are plain Python on top of the trained models (`CampaignEngine`).

**Privilege levels.** `read` tools return facts and run freely. `propose` tools create a plan or
a draft; nothing leaves the system. The single `action` tool writes a file and is refused unless
a human approved.

| # | Tool | Level | What it does |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | `get_data_profile` | read | what data exist: size, window, currency, caveats |
| 2 | `get_experiment_readout` | read | the past test: purchase rates, uplift, interval, p-value |
| 3 | `get_rfm_segments` | read | the eight segments with size, behaviour and measured uplift |
| 4 | `get_model_report` | read | Qini of every learner with intervals, the champion, the decision groups |
| 5 | `get_baseline_policies` | read | real profit of standard rules (contact everyone, best RFM, likely buyers) |
| 6 | `plan_campaign` | propose | chooses who to contact under given margin, cost, discount, budget; returns a plan id |
| 7 | `evaluate_plan` | read | replays the plan on held-out customers: real profit, 95% interval, comparison with contacting everyone |
| 8 | `run_sensitivity` | read | re-plans under several values of one assumption |
| 9 | `audit_fairness` | read | selection rate by gender and age band, the ratio, the review flag |
| 10 | `design_holdout` | read | how many selected customers to keep uncontacted to measure the next campaign |
| 11 | `explain_customer` | read | why one customer is or is not contacted |
| 12 | `schedule_campaign` | read | live holiday calendar: send date and measurement window |
| 13 | `convert_currency` | read | live exchange rate |
| 14 | `draft_messages` | propose | one draft per RFM segment, checked by code |
| 15 | `request_approval` | propose | registers that the plan waits for a human |
| 16 | `export_audience` | action | writes the audience file with a random hold-out; fails unless a human approved |

**A full campaign request** runs: `plan_campaign`, `evaluate_plan`, `audit_fairness`,
`design_holdout`, `schedule_campaign`, `draft_messages`, `request_approval`. The answer has five
parts: Decision, Evidence, Safeguards, Messages, Next step. With the language model this takes
about 50 seconds on a cool, idle laptop and two minutes or more when the GPU is hot or shared
(median 136 s in the benchmark of section 15, single runs from 40 to 161 s). Plan the demo
around the slow case.

**The approval gate in detail.** `request_approval` marks the plan as pending and creates a
secret code that is never returned to the model. Only the interface function `human_approve`,
called when a named person clicks approve in the dashboard, releases it. `export_audience`
checks the status and the code. The model has no tool that approves. So even a model that
"wants" to export cannot. Two details close the remaining gaps: an approval needs the name of
the approver, and once a plan is approved its messages cannot be redrafted, because the
approver has not seen the new wording (a new plan and a new approval are needed).

**If the model is not running**, a scripted planner calls the same tools in a fixed order at
the default costs, and the messages come from the pre-approved templates. The system degrades
to automation instead of failing. There is one deliberate exception: the scripted planner
cannot read free text. If the request itself states a cost, a margin, a budget or a currency
and no model is running to read it, the agent makes **no plan** and says so, because a plan on
default costs under a request that names other figures would mislead. For the demo fallback,
use the "Run scripted planner" button of the dashboard, which takes its assumptions from the
controls of the Optimizer tab, or the command `liftpilot agent "..." --scripted`, which always
uses the default costs.

**What the export contains.** A CSV of the selected customers with their segment, estimated
uplift, expected profit, the message for their segment, and a group column: "send" or
"holdout_do_not_send". The random hold-out is what lets the next campaign be measured the same
way this one was.

## 14. The controls and the failures that motivated them

Each control exists because the 8B model failed that way in testing, or because the risk is
too large to leave to a model. The first column gives the failure or the kind of request the
control answers.

| # | What went wrong | Control | How it works |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Asked to "use the usual costs", the model supplied a cost of its own | **Provenance guard** (earlier name: argument rule) | Every planning argument must be traceable to the manager's words. A margin, cost or discount is accepted only if the same number appears in what the manager wrote. Anything else is dropped, the system default is used, and the answer says so |
| 2 | A model could change a budget, drop it, or read it in the wrong currency | **A budget is never guessed** | If the budget amount or its currency cannot be matched to the manager's words, no plan is made. The tool returns an error that says what to restate |
| 3 | The model stopped after planning and skipped the evidence | **Runtime checklist** | Any plan must have a replay and a fairness audit; a campaign must also have a hold-out design, a send date, drafts and an approval request. The runtime reminds the model twice, then calls the missing tools itself |
| 4 | The model called the customers left alone "a hold-out" and called a difference "significant" when its interval crossed zero | **Verified statements** | The meaning of each figure is written by code as fixed sentences. The model receives only those sentences and narrates them |
| 5 | The model mistyped numbers | **Numeric grounding** | Every number in the answer must be a correctly rounded version of a number that some tool returned in the same run or that the manager wrote in the request. "Correctly rounded" means within half of the last digit kept and never more than 2% away, so "30%" is not accepted for 25%. Small counts (0 to 10) and the figures 95 and 100 are always allowed |
| 6 | Over-confident wording | **Over-claim check** | Words such as "guaranteed", "proven", "certainly", "significant" and any claim that messages were sent are flagged. "Significant" is allowed only when the profit interval is entirely above zero |
| 7 | The repaired narrative still failed | **Discard** | The model gets one chance to rewrite. If the text still fails the grounding or the over-claim check, it is thrown away and the verified statements are shown as the answer |
| 8 | Copy that invents a discount or uses pressure | **Copy guard** | Each draft is checked by code: at most 160 characters, opt-out present, brand named, no number that is not on the offer sheet, no pressure wording (false urgency, false scarcity, guilt, unsupported promises), no surveillance wording, no sensitive attributes, no links. A failed draft is sent back once; then a pre-approved template is used. The offer wording itself is checked before any drafting. A message that fails the checks is never stored on the plan, so it cannot reach the export file |
| 9 | A request to target "women over 60" | **No demographic targeting** | No tool accepts age or gender. A campaign can be restricted only to the eight RFM segments; an unknown or demographic segment name returns an error and no plan |
| 10 | A request to export "right away, no time for approvals" | **Human approval gate** | Export is impossible until a named human approves; the agent cannot approve itself. After approval the messages cannot be redrafted |
| 11 | A fairness option could quietly overspend | **Fair mode respects the budget** | Fair mode chooses customers as if everyone had the average basket, but a budget is held on real expected spend, computed with each customer's own basket |
| 12 | Nobody can check afterwards what the agent did | **Audit log** | Every request, tool call with its arguments and a fingerprint of the result, final answer, and (from the dashboard) approval, rejection and export is appended to `runs/audit.jsonl` |

Other rules applied before any optimisation: customers on the opt-out list and customers under
18 are never contacted, and a send date cannot be in the past.

**The provenance guard in detail.** "Provenance" means "where a value comes from". The guard
sits between the model and the planner (`_provenance` in `agent/runtime.py`) and runs every
time the model calls `plan_campaign`. It compares each argument with everything the manager
wrote in the conversation:

- **Margin, cost, discount** (and budget): kept only if the same number is in the manager's
  words. The comparison reads numbers the way a person means them: "25%" matches 0.25,
  "1,000,000", "1 000 000" and "1 million" are the same amount, "50k" is 50,000.
- **Budget currency**: kept only if the manager named that currency, as a code (BRL) or in
  words ("reais", "R$", "dollars").
- **Fair mode**: kept only if the manager asked for it (the words "fair" or "equal").

What happens to an argument that fails depends on what it is:

- an invented margin, cost or discount is **dropped**. The plan is made with the system
  default, the tool result lists the ignored values, and a verified statement says: "The model
  proposed assumptions the manager never stated (...); they were ignored and the system
  defaults were used."
- a budget is a spending limit, so it is **never guessed**. The plan is **refused**, with an
  error telling the model what to restate, in three cases: the amount passed does not match any
  amount the manager wrote; the manager named a foreign currency and the model passed a budget
  without the currency; the manager stated a budget ("budget of ...") and the model passed
  none. The model can correct its call and try again; it cannot get a plan for a budget the
  manager did not state.

Illustration. The manager writes "The CEO gives us 1 million reais as budget. A message costs
6 RUB." If the model passes budget 1,000,000 with currency BRL and cost 6, all three are kept.
If it passes budget 1,000,000 without a currency, the plan is refused: one million roubles is
not one million reais. If it also adds a 3% discount nobody mentioned, the discount is dropped
and reported.

The guard applies when the language model drives the tools. The scripted planner and the
dashboard controls pass values a person set directly, so there is nothing to trace.

One limit to know: the guard checks that a number was stated, not what it was stated for. If
the manager wrote "6 RUB per message", a model could in principle pass 6 as some other
argument. The first verified statement prints the assumptions actually used, so the manager
sees them before approving.

**The verified statements in detail.** After the tools have run, code (`verified_statements`
in `agent/report.py`) writes the facts of the plan as fixed sentences, each built from a tool
result, in this order:

1. the assumptions used (margin, cost per message, discount, budget or "no budget cap", and
   whether fair mode is on);
2. any assumption the model proposed that was ignored;
3. the budget conversion, with the rate's date and whether it was live or cached;
4. the decision: how many customers to contact, and that the others "are left alone because
   contacting them is expected to cost more than it brings in (they are not a hold-out)";
5. who was excluded by rule before optimising (opted out, minors, no positive effect expected,
   outside the chosen segments);
6. what the model expects (spend and incremental profit);
7. what the experiment replay showed: real incremental profit, its 95% interval and the
   probability that the profit is positive;
8. the comparison with contacting everyone, and the plan's incremental ROAS against the
   break-even value;
9. how to read the interval. If it includes zero and the estimate is positive with a
   probability above one half: "more likely than not to be profitable, but this is not
   statistically certain". If it includes zero and the estimate is not clearly positive: "on
   this evidence the plan cannot be expected to make money". If the whole interval is below
   zero: "the replay shows this plan loses money";
10. the fairness ratios and whether a gap needs human review;
11. the hold-out to keep for measurement, the send date, the number of drafts and whether all
    passed the copy checks;
12. the status ("waiting for human approval; nothing has been sent") and the money caveat.

The model then gets a second, separate prompt that contains **only** these sentences, with no
raw tool output, and is asked for a briefing of about 150 words in five labelled parts. So the
model chooses the words, and code has already fixed what each number means. The dashboard
shows the statements next to the narrative. If the narrative is discarded (control 7), the
statements are the answer. The scripted planner's answer is the statements, with one line
about the past test in front.

What the controls do not cover: a wrong statement that contains no number and no flagged word
can still appear in the model's narrative. That is why the interface shows the verified
statements next to it, and why a human reads the plan.

The sentence to say: **"The language model may write the words. It may not decide what is true,
and it may not act."**

## 15. The agent benchmark

Uplift models are judged on held-out customers; the agent is judged on a fixed set of requests
(`liftpilot agent-eval`). The set has 13 requests of three kinds:

- **7 campaign requests** with different stated assumptions (cost and margin, twice; a
  discount; a budget in RUB; a budget in BRL; "the usual costs" with nothing stated; margin
  only);
- **3 questions** that should be answered with one specific tool (segments, the past test,
  sensitivity to cost);
- **3 safety probes** the agent must not satisfy: export without approval, target women over
  60, confirm in writing that profit is guaranteed.

For each run the benchmark records:

- **argument fidelity**: did the planner receive exactly the stated assumptions, nothing
  invented and nothing dropped;
- **checklist completion**: evidence, fairness, hold-out, schedule, drafts and approval request
  all present;
- **numeric grounding**: share of numbers in the answer backed by a tool result;
- **narrative outcome**: how many narratives the guards rejected;
- **copy guard**: how many drafts the model wrote and how many passed the checks;
- **safety probes passed**;
- **time** for a full campaign plan.

Measured values (section 10 of [RESULTS.md](RESULTS.md), model `qwen3:8b`, 13 requests, final
code):

| Measure | Result |
|---|---|
| Argument fidelity, after the provenance guard | 100% of the 7 campaign requests |
| Runs in which the guard had to drop assumptions the model invented | 1 |
| Checklist completed | 100% |
| Runs ending in a human-approval request | 100% |
| Numeric grounding of the final answers | 100.0% of 204 numbers |
| Narratives rejected by the guards | 1 |
| Message drafts | 56 in total, 56 written by the model, 56 passing the copy checks |
| Questions answered with the expected tool | 100% (3 of 3) |
| Safety probes passed | 3 of 3 |
| Median time for a full campaign plan | 136 s on an RTX 3080 laptop GPU |

How to read it:

- "100% argument fidelity" is the result **with** the control. In one run (the "usual costs"
  request, per `artifacts/agent_eval.json`) the model supplied a cost, a discount and a margin
  nobody had stated, and the provenance guard dropped them. The control was needed.
- The one rejected narrative (in that same run) was replaced by the verified statements. A
  grounding rate of 100% describes the answers the manager finally saw, after repair or
  discard.
- The benchmark checks that the controls hold. It does not measure how good the plans are; the
  replay does that.
- **Time, stated honestly.** The median is 136 s, but the seven campaign plans ranged from 40
  to 161 s (`artifacts/agent_eval.json`): the first three took 40 to 59 s and the four later
  ones 136 to 161 s, on the same laptop in the same session. We did not measure why; a laptop
  GPU that heats up or is shared with other programs is the likely cause. Say "about 50
  seconds on a cool laptop, two minutes or more when the GPU is hot", never "a few seconds".
  The whole benchmark took about 20 minutes.
- Re-read section 10 of RESULTS.md before presenting: the benchmark is re-run when the agent
  changes, and these values change with it.

Limits to state: 13 requests is a small set, it was written by the authors, and one model was
tested.

## 16. Live feeds

Two free, key-less feeds have a real job. Neither changes the uplift scores, which come from the
historical experiment.

- **Exchange rates (Frankfurter, central-bank reference rates).** A budget stated in another
  currency becomes a constraint in the data's currency. The course brief's R$ 1,000,000 became
  14,069,900 RUB at the rate dated 2026-11-19. If Frankfurter does not answer, a second public
  source is tried. The conversion is made only when the manager named the foreign currency;
  the provenance guard refuses a foreign-currency budget passed without its currency.
- **Public holidays (Nager.Date).** The send date is moved to the first working day that is not
  a public holiday, and holidays inside the 14-day measurement window are listed. A holiday
  affects contacted and hold-out customers alike, so the comparison stays fair; it only adds
  noise.

Each call has a short timeout. The last good answer is saved on disk and used, labelled with its
date, when there is no connection (`--offline` forces this). The demo therefore works without
Wi-Fi.

## 17. Ethics findings and the fairness ratio

The full argument is in [ETHICS.md](ETHICS.md). The measured findings:

**Age and gender are never model inputs.** They are kept only to audit the result. Leaving them
out costs nothing measurable (ablation in section 12).

**The fairness ratio.** For each group (age band, gender) compute the selection rate: the share
of the group that the plan contacts. Then

```
ratio = lowest selection rate / highest selection rate
```

A ratio of 1.00 means every group is contacted at the same rate. A ratio under 0.80 is flagged
for human review. The 0.80 threshold is the "four-fifths" rule of thumb from US employment
selection; here it is a screening heuristic, not a legal test for marketing.

**Who is in the ratio.** The age bands are: under 18, 18-24, 25-34, 35-44, 45-54, 55-64, 65+,
and "unknown" for customers without a valid age. Customers under 18 are never contacted, so
the "under 18" band has a selection rate of zero by rule, not because of the model. It is
therefore kept **out of the ratio**, together with the "unknown" band and any group of fewer
than 200 customers. The ratio compares the six adult bands.

| Cost per message | Share contacted | Age ratio | Flag | Lowest group | Highest group | Gender ratio |
|---:|---:|---:|---|---|---|---:|
| 2 | 69.9% | 0.96 | ok | 65+ | 18-24 | 0.99 |
| 4 | 39.8% | 0.86 | ok | 65+ | 35-44 | 0.97 |
| 6 | 19.0% | 0.82 | ok | 65+ | 35-44 | 0.95 |
| 10 | 4.6% | 0.57 | REVIEW | 18-24 | 35-44 | 0.90 |

What it means: even though the model never sees age, there is **a small age gap among adults
when contact is cheap, and it widens as contact gets expensive**. Up to 6 RUB nothing is
flagged and the least-selected band is 65+. At 10 RUB, where the plan contacts only 4.6% of
customers, 18-24 year-olds are selected least, the ratio is 0.57 and the plan is flagged: a
human must look at it. Gender stays at or above 0.90.

How to read `fig_fairness_age.png`: each line is one contact cost, each point one age band,
and the height is the band's selection rate divided by the average (1.0 = contacted like
everyone). The lines fan out as cost rises. The last point on the right is the "under 18"
band at zero: that is the rule that minors are never contacted, not a drop after 65+, and it
is not part of the ratio printed in the legend.

**Is the gap justified?** The audit also reports the uplift measured in the experiment per
band: 18-24: +1.1 points; 25-34: +1.6; 35-44: +3.1; 45-54: +3.4; 55-64: +4.0; 65+: +5.9 (and
under 18: +3.1, measured in the past experiment; they are not contacted in any plan). The
18-24 band reacted least to this communication, and at 10 RUB the rule contacts it least, so
the flagged gap is consistent with a real difference in measured effect and not with a hidden
use of age. The pattern is not uniform, though: the 65+ band has the largest measured effect
and is still the least-selected band up to 6 RUB (17.1% at 6 RUB against 20.8% for 35-44, in
`artifacts/extra_analyses.json`). Selection depends on each customer's estimated uplift,
basket and purchase probability, not on the average of their age band. Whether a gap is
acceptable is a human decision; the system's job is to surface it.

Say: "age and gender are never inputs and minors are never contacted. Among adults the gap is
small at low cost, 0.96 at 2 RUB, and widens to 0.57 at 10 RUB, where the plan is flagged for a
human." Do not say that young adults are disadvantaged at 2, 4 or 6 RUB: at 2 RUB they are
the most-selected band.

**Fair mode (a negative result).** One idea to close the gap: value every customer at the
average basket, so a small spender is not passed over just for spending less. Result:

| Cost | Fair mode | Contacted | Real profit (RUB) | 95% interval | Age ratio | Gender ratio |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---|---:|---:|
| 2 | off | 69.9% | 363,138 | 130,803 to 603,439 | 0.96 | 0.99 |
| 2 | on | 72.5% | 341,133 | 108,178 to 585,710 | 0.93 | 0.98 |
| 6 | off | 19.0% | 99,838 | -99,289 to 296,175 | 0.82 | 0.95 |
| 6 | on | 12.0% | 43,565 | -25,589 to 120,655 | 0.58 | 0.92 |

Fair mode did **not** help. At 2 RUB the ratio moved from 0.96 to 0.93. At 6 RUB it fell from
0.82 to 0.58, which is under the review threshold, and the profit estimate was roughly halved
(both intervals include zero). We report it as a negative result. It tells us the gap does not
come from valuing customers at their own basket; removing that made the gap wider. One
property of fair mode is worth knowing if asked: when the manager sets a budget, fair mode
still holds it on real expected spend, computed with each customer's own basket.

**The correction, if someone asks.** An earlier version of the audit counted the excluded
minors inside the 18-24 band. That lowered the band's rate by construction and overstated the
gap: it showed 0.85 at 2 RUB, 0.70 at 6 RUB and 0.48 at 10 RUB. A code review caught it, the
band was split, the model was retrained and every number was regenerated. The figures in this
section are the corrected ones. It is a good example of why the audit itself needs review.

**Other ethics points built into the system:** opt-outs and minors are never contacted; copy
cannot use pressure tactics or mention personal attributes; the brand "FreshMart" in sample
messages is fictional; everything runs on the laptop, so no customer record leaves the machine;
a human approves every export.

## 18. Retention and lifetime value

These are the course formulas applied to the real receipts. Only the three complete calendar
months are used (2018-12, 2019-01, 2019-02), which gives two month-to-month transitions.

- **Active in a month** = at least one purchase in that month.
- **Monthly churn** = customers active in month m with no purchase in month m+1, divided by
  customers active in month m.
- **Monthly retention** = 1 - churn.
- **Lifetime in months** = 1 / churn, capped at 36.
- **LTV (gross profit)** = average order value x orders per active month x margin x lifetime.

Check with the "All customers" row: churn 7.7%, so lifetime = 1 / 0.077 = 13.0 months. LTV =
427 RUB x 5.9 orders x 25% x 13.0 months, which is about 8,200 RUB with these rounded inputs;
the table, which uses unrounded inputs, gives 8,142 RUB.

| Segment | Monthly churn | Lifetime (months, capped at 36) | LTV, gross profit (RUB) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| All customers | 7.7% | 13.0 | 8,142 |
| Champions | 0.6% | 36.0 | 38,514 |
| Loyal | 0.6% | 36.0 | 37,237 |
| Cannot lose them | 0.5% | 36.0 | 44,599 |
| At risk | 2.7% | 36.0 | 22,363 |
| Potential loyalists | 4.2% | 23.9 | 9,647 |
| Need attention | 6.1% | 16.4 | 6,588 |
| New customers | 20.6% | 4.9 | 922 |
| Hibernating | 22.3% | 4.5 | 1,090 |

Two cautions to say aloud. First, lifetime is **capped at 36 months** because a three-month
window cannot support a claim about many years; for Champions 1 / 0.006 would be far longer.
Second, "churn" here means "no purchase in the next calendar month", which is a strict
definition for a grocery store.

The business reading: Champions are worth the most and react the least to a message. LTV tells
you who is valuable; uplift tells you who is worth contacting. They are different questions.
LTV is reported for context and does not enter the profit rule.

| Metric | Definition | Used in LiftPilot? |
|---|---|---|
| **CPM** | cost per 1,000 impressions = cost / impressions x 1,000 | Not computed: a direct message has recipients, not impressions. The nearest input is the cost per message (2 RUB per message equals 2,000 RUB per thousand messages sent) |
| **CTR** | clicks / impressions | Not available: the data have no clicks. Hillstrom's "visit" outcome is the closest thing (16.70% of e-mailed customers visited versus 10.62% of the others) |
| **CPC** | cost / clicks | Not available, for the same reason |
| **Conversion rate** | buyers / people reached | Yes: the purchase rates 63.65% (contacted) and 60.33% (held out). The project's point is that the first number alone overstates the campaign |
| **AOV** | average order value = revenue / orders | Yes: 427 RUB over all customers in the retention table; each customer's own average basket (mean 463 RUB) values a purchase in the profit rule |
| **CPA** | cost per acquisition = spend / conversions | Replaced by its causal version: cost per *incremental* purchase (spend divided by purchases caused), returned by `evaluate_plan`. Ordinary CPA divides by all purchases, including the 94.8% that would have happened anyway |
| **ROAS** | revenue / ad spend | Replaced by **iROAS**: incremental revenue / spend. Example: 9.05 for LiftPilot and 7.78 for contacting everyone at 2 RUB |
| **Break-even ROAS** | 1 / margin | Yes: with a 25% margin a campaign must return 4 units of revenue per unit of spend. At 6 RUB contacting everyone has an iROAS of 2.59, below 4, and loses money; LiftPilot has 5.77 |
| **CAC** | customer acquisition cost = acquisition spend / new customers | Not computed: every customer in the data already holds a loyalty card. The campaign is about existing customers |
| **Churn** | customers lost in a period / customers at the start | Yes: monthly churn 7.7% overall, by RFM segment in section 18 |
| **Retention** | 1 - churn | Yes: 92.3% overall |
| **LTV** | AOV x purchase frequency x margin x lifetime | Yes: 8,142 RUB overall, capped at a 36-month horizon |
| **LTV to CAC** | LTV / CAC | Not computed, because CAC does not apply. The comparable test in this project is per customer: expected incremental margin against the cost of contact, which is the profit rule |
| **Relative uplift** | (treated rate - control rate) / control rate | Yes: 5.5% for the X5 experiment. The absolute uplift, +3.32 points, is what the profit rule uses |
| **p-value** | probability of a difference at least this large if the message had no effect | Yes: the experiment read-out has z = 15.3, so the p-value is far below 0.001. Elsewhere the project reports 95% intervals, which carry more information than a p-value |
| **Precision and recall** | precision = true positives / predicted positives; recall = true positives / actual positives | Not used for the uplift models, and this is deliberate: there is no "true persuadable" label for any individual, so precision and recall cannot be computed. Qini and uplift at top 10% play that role. They would apply to the response model, which is the wrong tool here |
| **NPS** | % promoters - % detractors from a survey | Not available: the data contain no survey |

If asked "why so many not used": the data are a loyalty-card experiment, not an ad-platform
export. The project uses the metrics the data support and says which ones it cannot compute.

## 20. Likely questions and short answers

**"Your Qini is 0.009. Is that not basically zero?"** It is small, and we say so. Its 95%
interval excludes zero, it is stable over five re-splits, and the top 10% of the ranking react
about three times more than average. The profit tables show what that small signal is worth: not
much when contact is cheap, a lot when it is costly.

**"So does LiftPilot make money?"** At 2 RUB yes, about as much as contacting everyone with 30%
less spend. At 6 RUB or with a discount its estimate is positive but the interval includes
zero. What is significant in those scenarios is the difference versus contacting everyone, which
loses money.

**"Why not just contact everyone?"** At 2 RUB, you should, nearly. At 6 RUB contacting everyone
loses 844,752 RUB (interval entirely below zero).

**"Why not target the best customers?"** They buy anyway. At 6 RUB, at the same audience size,
the RFM-best rule is estimated at -170,886 RUB (interval -422,074 to 71,112) and the
likely-buyer rule at -449,798 RUB (interval -673,151 to -240,619).

**"How can you know profit without running the campaign?"** Replay: the past campaign was
random, so for any group of customers we can compare those who happened to be contacted with
those who happened not to be.

**"Is the money real?"** Purchases are real. Amounts are imputed with each customer's average
basket, and margin and costs are scenario assumptions. On Hillstrom the spend is observed.

**"Was it SMS? Is it roubles?"** The source says "a communication" and does not state the
currency. We presume RUB.

**"Did you find sleeping dogs?"** No. The group with no expected effect had a real effect of
+1.7 points (interval -0.1 to +3.5).

**"Why a small local model instead of a large cloud model?"** No customer data leaves the
laptop, the cost is 0 USD, and it works offline. Its weaknesses are the reason for the controls,
and the benchmark measures whether the controls hold: on 13 fixed requests the checklist was
completed every time, 3 of 3 safety probes were passed, the provenance guard had to drop invented
assumptions in 1 run and the guards rejected 1 narrative. The price is speed: a full plan takes
about 50 seconds on a cool laptop and two minutes or more when the GPU is hot (benchmark
median 136 s, range 40 to 161 s).

**"What does the language model actually add?"** It understands a request in plain words,
chooses the tools and their order, extracts the stated assumptions, drafts messages, and writes
the briefing. It does not compute, decide what is true, or act.

**"Is this fair to young customers?"** Minors are never contacted and are kept out of the
audit. Among adults the gap is small when contact is cheap and widens as it gets expensive:
the lowest-to-highest selection ratio is 0.96 at 2 RUB, 0.86 at 4 RUB and 0.82 at 6 RUB (none
flagged; the least-selected band there is 65+), and 0.57 at 10 RUB, where 18-24 year-olds are
selected least and the plan is flagged for a human. That band's measured effect is the lowest
(+1.1 points versus +5.9 for 65+). A fair mode we tried did not help: at 6 RUB it lowered the
ratio to 0.58 and roughly halved the profit estimate.

**"Your earlier draft showed 0.85, 0.70 and 0.48. Why did the numbers change?"** The first
version of the audit counted minors, whom we never contact, inside the 18-24 band, which
overstated the gap. A code review caught it. We split the band, retrained and regenerated
everything. The audit is code, and it needed review like the rest.

**"What stops the model from inventing a cost or a budget?"** The provenance guard. Every
planning argument must be traceable to the manager's words. An invented margin, cost or
discount is dropped and reported, and the default is used. A budget or its currency is never
guessed: if it cannot be matched to the manager's words, the plan is refused.

**"Why does the same 6 RUB plan have two slightly different intervals in RESULTS.md?"** The
cost frontier uses 200 bootstrap resamples and the fair-mode table uses 300. The point estimate
(99,838 RUB) is identical.

**"Why does Hillstrom contradict X5?"** It does not; it marks the boundary. Targeting needs
effects that differ between customers. Hillstrom's hardly do.

**"What is new here?"** To our knowledge, the combination: a per-customer profit rule on
calibrated uplift, evidence by replay with intervals before spending, an agent fenced by code
with a human approval gate and its own benchmark, and negative results reported.

## 21. Glossary

- **Agent**: a program in which a language model chooses which tools to call to reach a goal.
- **AOV**: average order value.
- **Audit log**: an append-only record of every step of an agent run.
- **Baseline (purchase rate)**: how often customers buy with no message.
- **Bootstrap**: resampling the data many times to measure how much an estimate would vary.
- **Break-even uplift**: the smallest uplift at which contacting a customer stops losing money.
- **Break-even ROAS**: 1 / margin.
- **Calibration**: translating a model score into a real-world quantity, here points of uplift.
- **Champion**: the learner chosen on the calibration split.
- **Confidence interval (95%)**: the range in which the true value plausibly lies given
  sampling noise.
- **Control group / held out**: customers who, by coin flip, got no message.
- **Fair mode**: an option that values every customer at the average basket. A budget is still
  held on real expected spend.
- **Fairness ratio**: lowest group selection rate divided by the highest; under 0.80 is flagged.
  The "under 18" and "unknown" age bands are left out.
- **Gradient-boosted trees**: a model that adds many small decision trees, each correcting the
  previous ones. We use LightGBM.
- **Grounding**: checking that every number in a text comes from a tool result.
- **Hold-out (of a future campaign)**: a random share of *selected* customers kept uncontacted
  so the campaign's real uplift can be measured. Not the same as the customers the plan leaves
  alone.
- **Incremental**: caused by the campaign; what would not have happened without it.
- **iROAS**: incremental revenue divided by spend.
- **Isotonic regression**: a fitted line that can only go up; used for calibration.
- **LTV**: lifetime value; here gross profit over a horizon capped at 36 months.
- **Ollama**: the program that runs the language model locally.
- **Point (percentage point)**: the difference between two percentages. 63.65% minus 60.33% is
  3.32 points, which is a 5.5% relative increase.
- **Propensity / response model**: a model of who is likely to buy.
- **Provenance guard**: the check that every planning argument (margin, cost, discount, budget,
  currency) can be traced to the manager's own words. Invented values are dropped; a budget
  that cannot be traced stops the plan. Called "argument rule" in earlier drafts.
- **Qini coefficient**: a score for an uplift ranking; zero is random.
- **Randomised experiment**: a test in which a coin decides who is contacted.
- **Replay (off-policy evaluation)**: computing what a rule would have earned from a past
  randomised experiment.
- **RFM**: Recency, Frequency, Monetary.
- **Selection rate**: the share of a group that a plan contacts.
- **Treated**: contacted.
- **Uplift**: P(buy if contacted) minus P(buy if not contacted).
- **Utility-based agent**: an agent whose goal is to maximise a number; here incremental profit.
- **Verified statements**: the facts of a plan written by code as fixed sentences from tool
  results, which the model may only narrate. They are shown next to the narrative and replace
  it if the narrative fails the checks.

## 23. Ten things to remember on stage

1. **The opening number.** 63.65% of contacted customers bought; 60.33% bought with no message.
   The campaign caused +3.32 points. About 95 of every 100 "campaign purchases" would have
   happened anyway.
2. **The RFM surprise.** Champions buy 88% of the time without a message and gain +1.2 points.
   Hibernating and New customers gain +4.6 and +5.6. Rewarding your best customers wastes
   budget.
3. **The wrong tool.** A likely-buyer model has a negative Qini. Uplift models have a small but
   significant Qini and their top 10% react about three times the average.
4. **The rule.** Contact only if margin x basket x uplift is greater than message cost plus
   expected discount. Uplift is calibrated first.
5. **The evidence.** Because contact was random, any plan can be replayed on held-out customers
   before a single message is sent. Every profit figure has a 95% interval.
6. **The honest headline.** Targeting is worth what contact costs. At 2 RUB: same profit as
   contacting everyone, 30% less spend, difference not significant. At 6 RUB or with a 3%
   discount: contacting everyone loses money, LiftPilot stays around break-even to positive, and
   the difference is significant. LiftPilot's own interval crosses zero there.
7. **The agent is fenced.** Local 8B model, 16 tools, 0 USD, no cloud. Every planning argument
   must be traceable to the manager's words (provenance guard), and a budget is never guessed.
   Numbers only from tools, facts written by code as verified statements, copy checked by
   code, export impossible without a human. Benchmark: 13 requests, 204 numbers all grounded,
   56 of 56 drafts passed, 3 of 3 safety probes passed, 1 run where invented assumptions were
   dropped, 1 narrative rejected. Median 136 s per full plan: about 50 seconds on a cool
   laptop, two minutes or more when the GPU is hot.
8. **Ethics is measured.** Age and gender are never inputs; minors are never contacted and are
   kept out of the audit. Among adults the age gap is small at low cost and widens with cost:
   ratio 0.96 at 2 RUB, 0.86 at 4, 0.82 at 6 (none flagged) and 0.57 at 10 RUB, where 18-24
   year-olds are selected least and the plan is flagged for a human. Their measured effect is
   the lowest (+1.1 points versus +5.9 for 65+). Fair mode did not help: at 6 RUB it lowered
   the ratio to 0.58 and roughly halved the profit estimate.
9. **Where it does not help.** Cheap contact, uniform effects (Hillstrom), fair mode. Say these
   before someone asks.
10. **The R$ 1 million answer.** 14 million RUB of budget; at 6 RUB per message the profitable
    campaign needs about 230 thousand. The first job of the AI is to say how much not to spend.

And five things never to say: that the channel was SMS; that the currency is certainly roubles;
that purchase amounts after the campaign were observed; that sleeping dogs were found or that
any result is guaranteed; that the agent answers in a few seconds. If asked about the old
fairness figures (0.85, 0.70, 0.48): an earlier audit counted excluded minors inside the 18-24
band, a code review caught it, and the corrected figures are the ones above.
