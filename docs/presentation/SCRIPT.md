## The script, slide by slide

### Slide 1 (0:15): Title

Hello everyone. I am Abed, presenting for Group 10 with Ghandi. LiftPilot is an AI campaign agent that spends a marketing budget only where it changes what a customer does.

### Slide 2 (0:50): Did the ads make money? About 95 of every 100 campaign purchases would have happened anyway.

This course asks one question about every campaign: did the ads make money? Here is a real answer. A grocery chain, X5, sent a message to half of 200 thousand loyalty customers, chosen at random. Of those contacted, 63.65 percent bought. Of those left alone, 60.33 percent bought anyway. So the message caused 3.3 points. Look at the dots. Of every 100 purchases after the message, about 95 would have happened without it. A dashboard counts all 100 as campaign results. Only about 5 were caused.

### Slide 3 (0:45): The CEO's R$ 1 million question has an uncomfortable first answer: spend far less of it.

Our brief was the AI consultant exercise: a CEO gives us one million reais for one AI initiative, and we answer six questions. They are on the slide; here are two. Value: when a message costs 6 roubles, our plan earns about one million roubles more than contacting everyone. And the uncomfortable part: one million reais is about 14 million roubles, but the plan the system recommends spends only about 230 thousand. So the first job of the AI is to say how much not to spend.

### Slide 4 (0:40): LiftPilot finds the people who buy because you contacted them, and proves it first.

The solution in one sentence. Most marketing AI finds people who will buy. LiftPilot finds people who will buy because you contacted them, proves it on a real experiment, and asks a human before anything is sent. Other projects in this class predict, segment or measure. Ours differs on four points: it decides who not to contact, it proves each plan before money is spent, it fences the agent with controls we measured, and it reports where it does not help.

### Slide 5 (0:35): Everything rests on real public data, and we say what the data does not tell us.

The data is real and public. X5: 400 thousand customers and 45.8 million receipt lines, with the random test inside. The Hillstrom e-mail test is our outside check. What we do not know: the source names neither the channel nor the currency, so we presume roubles, and it gives no purchase amounts after the campaign, so we use each customer's average basket.

### Slide 6 (0:30): Eight steps turn raw receipts into an audience file, with a human gate before any action.

The pipeline. From receipts we compute RFM and behaviour features, without age or gender. Uplift models estimate what a message changes for each customer. A profit rule decides who to contact, and the plan is replayed on customers the models never saw. The agent, in purple, drives these steps through tools. A human approves, in red; only then is an audience file written.

### Slide 7 (0:55): RFM shows who is persuadable: the best customers react least to a message.

RFM means recency, frequency and monetary value, each scored from 1 to 5. The weighted score uses 0.40, 0.35 and 0.25. Take a customer scored 5, 2 and 3: 2 plus 0.7 plus 0.75 gives 3.45. Those weights are our assumption, not an estimate. Now the chart: how each segment really reacted. Champions buy 88 percent of the time with no message, so a message adds only 1.2 points. Hibernating customers gain 4.6 and new customers 5.6. Rewarding your best customers wastes budget.

### Slide 8 (0:45): A likely-buyer model ranks the wrong people; uplift models find those a message changes.

The first AI part. The usual tool is a likely-buyer model: predict who will purchase and contact the top of the list. We tested it with Qini, which counts the extra purchases a ranking finds compared with random targeting. The likely-buyer model, in red, is no better than random. Uplift models learn from both groups of the experiment, so they learn the difference a message makes. Their Qini is small but significantly above zero, and their top 10 percent reacts by 9.9 points, three times the average.

### Slide 9 (1:00): One rule decides each customer: contact only if the extra margin beats the cost.

The calculation, for one made-up customer. Margin 25 percent, basket 400 roubles, message cost 6. She buys 40 percent of the time without a message and 48 percent with one: uplift 8 points. Extra margin is a quarter of 400 times 0.08: 8 roubles. Cost 6. Profit plus 2, so we contact her. Her break-even uplift is 6 points. Incremental ROAS, counting only caused revenue, is 32 over 6, about 5.3; break-even is 1 over margin, so 4. Cost per incremental purchase, the honest CAC, is 75 roubles against 100 of margin. Add a 3 percent discount and her cost becomes 11.76: we skip her.

### Slide 10 (0:50): Targeting is worth what contact costs: no gain at 2 RUB, a significant gain at 6 RUB.

The results, replayed on held-out customers. The headline: targeting is worth what contact costs. In panel A a message costs 2 roubles. Contacting almost everyone is right; LiftPilot matches it with about 30 percent less spend, and the difference is not significant. In panel B, at 6 roubles, contacting everyone loses about 845 thousand roubles. LiftPilot is about one million better, and that is significant. With a discount the gap is 2.8 million. One caution: LiftPilot's own profit interval crosses zero in the costly scenarios. And costs and margin are our assumptions, not facts about X5.

### Slide 11 (0:30): Retention and lifetime value differ sharply by segment, on only three months of data.

Retention and lifetime value, with the course formulas on the real receipts. Monthly churn is 7.7 percent overall. Champions almost never leave; hibernating and new customers churn above 20 percent a month. The segments worth the most are the ones a message changes least. The caveat: three full months of data, so read these as orders of magnitude.

### Slide 12 (0:45): The agent is a local language model fenced by 16 tools, coded checks and a human gate.

The second AI part is the agent. In the course taxonomy it is utility-based: it looks for the plan with the highest expected profit. Its brain is an 8-billion-parameter language model on this laptop, with no cloud and no fee. It can act only through 16 tools: 12 read, 3 propose, 1 acts. In testing the model invented arguments, skipped steps and mistyped numbers. Each failure became a control, on the right. And the export tool refuses to run until a named human approves.

### Slide 13 (0:50): Machine learning scores, rules decide, the language model explains, a human approves.

So where does the AI actually work? Machine learning estimates uplift. Rules decide. Statistics prove the plan. The language model chooses tools, explains and drafts messages. Code checks its wording, and a human approves. We benchmarked the agent on 13 fixed requests. After our guard, the planner received exactly the stated assumptions in every campaign run. Every checklist was completed. All 203 numbers in its answers traced to a tool result, and it passed 3 of 3 safety traps. A full plan takes about 149 seconds, which is why I started it early.

### Slide 15 (0:55): Is it wise? We measured an age gap, tried to fix it, and report that the fix failed.

Is it wise? Age and gender are never model inputs, yet we audited who gets selected. As contact gets more expensive, 18 to 24 year-olds are selected less: the ratio falls from 0.85 to 0.70, then 0.48, and the system flags it for human review. The gap follows a real difference: the message moves the youngest by 1.4 points and the over-65s by 5.9. We tried a fair mode. It did not close the gap and roughly halved profit, so we report a negative result. The legal texts behind the controls are on the slide.

### Slide 16 (0:45): A real business can pilot this in 12 weeks, if contact costs money and it can run one random test.

Could it help a real business? Yes, within limits. It helps when contact costs real money or carries a discount, and when customers react differently. It does not help when contact is nearly free or everyone reacts the same, as our second dataset showed. A retailer needs its receipts and one random test. This twelve-week plan runs that test first, then trains and audits the model, and ends with a pilot and a go or no-go decision. Our limits: one campaign, small effects, imputed purchase amounts.

### Backup slide 1: model table (only if asked)

Backup, only if asked. Seven models on the same 40,008 test customers. The likely-buyer model has a negative Qini. All six uplift models, five learners and their ensemble, are positive with intervals above zero. The champion, the S-learner, was chosen on the calibration split before we looked at the test results. The intervals overlap, so we do not claim one uplift learner beats another.

### Backup slide 2: Hillstrom (only if asked)

Backup, only if asked. On the Hillstrom e-mail test the effect is nearly the same for everyone, and the uplift rankings are unstable across re-splits. So the right answer is all or nothing. The profit rule finds the switch: send to all at 10 cents, to nobody at 60 cents. In the discount scenario it loses to sending to everyone, by an amount that is not significant. Targeting only helps where effects really differ between customers.
